# halo-record and the EU AI Act's record-keeping articles

Where halo-record's output sits against the EU AI Act's logging obligations for high-risk AI systems — stated conservatively: this maps *artifacts to articles*, it does not claim compliance for anyone, and using halo-record makes no one compliant with anything. Compliance is a property of the provider's or deployer's whole program; a record format is one exhibit in it.

**Not affiliated with the European Commission or any EU body. Not legal advice.** Article texts below are quoted from the Act; verify against the current consolidated text — dates have moved before (the substance of the logging articles has not).

## The articles this speaks to

- **Article 12(1)** — high-risk AI systems "shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs)" over the lifetime of the system. This is the *capability* obligation: the system must be able to produce event logs automatically.
- **Article 19** — providers keep the logs generated by their high-risk systems, to the extent such logs are under their control.
- **Article 26(6)** — deployers keep the logs generated by their high-risk system, for a period appropriate to the system's purpose and **at least six months**, unless other law provides otherwise. Note who this lands on: the *buyer* of the system, not only its maker.

Application dates for the high-risk obligations (post-amendment): December 2, 2027 for Annex III stand-alone systems; August 2, 2028 for systems embedded in regulated products. The dates have shifted; the logging text has not.

## What halo-record produces against them

| Obligation | What a Runtime Record chain provides | What it does not provide |
|---|---|---|
| Art 12(1) automatic recording of events | Per-action records written automatically at the instrumented boundary — tool calls, model calls, approvals — timestamped, append-only, hash-chained | Whether *your* capture surface satisfies "events" for *your* system is a scoping judgment (LIMITS §4); the Act's Annex-specific event minimums are the provider's analysis |
| Art 19 / Art 26(6) log retention | Plain JSONL files either party can retain on their own infrastructure; [RETENTION.md](RETENTION.md) covers operating them under a retention window, including checkpoint-boundary pruning with RFC 3161 timestamps | No retention enforcement ships; the ≥6-month clock is your policy, on your files |
| Deployer-side custody (Art 26(6)) | The verifiable-by-anyone property matters here: a deployer holding a vendor's chain can verify integrity without the vendor's help, and a witness checkpoint makes later alteration of the retained logs detectable | A self-held chain alone does not prove completeness (LIMITS §1) — the Act does not ask for that property, but your reviewer might |

## Known gaps, stated plainly

- The Act's logging provisions specify *what* must be recordable for particular Annex III systems (e.g., periods of use, reference databases checked, input data) in ways that are system-specific. halo-record is a general per-action record format; mapping your system's Annex-specific event list onto record fields is design work this document does not do for you.
- Nothing here covers the Act's non-logging obligations (risk management, data governance, human oversight, transparency) — different controls, different tools.

## The one-line version

The Act asks that high-risk systems *technically allow automatic event recording* and that both providers and deployers *keep the logs*. A hash-chained, automatically-written, retainable-by-either-party record file is that artifact class — and the tamper-evidence is the part the article text does not require but every party relying on retained logs eventually wants.
